# Session 3 notes · The seesaw rig

## 1. From thrust to torque

A quadrotor rolls by making the rotors on one side push harder than those on the other.
The rig does exactly this with two motors at distance $l$ from the pivot:

$$J\ddot\theta = l\,(T_1 - T_2) - m g h\sin\theta - b\dot\theta ,$$

with $\theta$ the beam angle (positive: motor-1 end up), $J$ the inertia about the pivot,
$m$ the moving mass, $h$ the distance of its centre of gravity **below** the pivot and $b$ a
viscous friction coefficient.

| $h$ | behaviour without control | like |
|---|---|---|
| $h > 0$ | stable pendulum, period $2\pi\sqrt{J/(mgh)}$ | a gentle first test |
| $h = 0$ | neutral: any angle is an equilibrium | a drone in hover |
| $h < 0$ | falls over | an inverted pendulum |

## 2. Motors, ESCs and the mixer

An ESC reads a pulse of 1000-2000 µs repeated every frame (250 Hz in the lab sketches,
generated by Timer1 so the width is exact) and spins the motor accordingly. With throttle
$x = (\text{us} - 1000)/1000$, the static thrust of a propeller is close to quadratic in
speed, and speed roughly follows throttle, so

$$T(x) \approx c_1 x + c_2 x^2 ,$$

reached through a lag $\tau_m$ (motor and propeller inertia, roughly 50-100 ms for a
10-inch propeller). Lab 3 measures $c_1$, $c_2$ with a kitchen scale: rest the motor-2 end on
the tared scale and spin motor 1 only; with equal arms the moment balance about the pivot
says the reading rises by exactly $T_1$.

**Mixer.** The controller produces one number $u$ (µs): $\text{us}_{1,2} = \text{base} \pm u$. Near
the limits, plain clipping would shrink $\text{us}_1 - \text{us}_2$ exactly when the controller asks
for more torque; the lab mixer shifts both commands instead (Betaflight calls this air
mode) and limits $u$ only when $2|u|$ exceeds the whole range.

**Linearisation.** Around the base throttle $x_0$ a small $u$ changes the torque by

$$\tau = k_u u, \qquad k_u = 2l\,\frac{c_1 + 2c_2x_0}{1000}\ \ \text{N m per µs}.$$

With the model defaults ($c_1 = 1.6$ N, $c_2 = 6.4$ N, $l = 0.25$ m, base 1350 µs):
$k_u \approx 3.0\times10^{-3}$ N m/µs, so 100 µs of differential gives about 24 rad/s² on
$J = 0.0126$ kg m².

## 3. The linear model

States: angle, rate and motor torque (first-order lag):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\theta\\ \omega\\ \tau\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 & 0\\ -mgh/J & -b/J & 1/J\\ 0 & 0 & -1/\tau_m\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\theta\\ \omega\\ \tau\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\k_u/\tau_m\end{bmatrix}u .$$

`bootcamp.rig.linear_model(params, base_us)` returns these matrices; `bootcamp.rig.Rig`
simulates the nonlinear rig with sensor noise, pot quantisation and the stops.

## 4. Why P alone fails

With $u = -K_p\theta$ the closed loop is third order: a lightly damped pendulum with a motor
lag. Raising $K_p$ raises the stiffness but every extra radian per second of bandwidth meets
more phase lag from the motor, and the oscillation grows. In the model P-only control
becomes unstable at about $K_p \approx 0.8$ µs/deg (notebook 3, section 3). A derivative term
supplies the damping: with $K_d = 0.3$ µs/(deg/s) the loop stays stable over the whole range of
$K_p$ tried. This is the single most important lesson for drones: **attitude control needs
rate feedback**. In session 3 the rate comes from differencing the pot; in session 4 from a gyro.

The easyPID derivative is filtered (`setDerivativeFilter(FILTER_EMA, 0.6)` in the sketch;
at 200 Hz that is a cut-off of about 16 Hz) because differencing a 0.26°-per-count pot
reading at 200 Hz produces steps of 50 deg/s.

## 5. Design in simulation, then walk the gains up

Notebook 3 finds gains that meet a step specification on the model, including the case
$h = 0$ (you will not know $h$ exactly). Those gains are a **target**, not the first thing to
type on the rig: every model parameter is an assumption until measured, and the model has
no vibration, no ESC low-throttle quirks and no beam flex. The lab procedure:

1. start at `kp 0.5 ki 0 kd 0.3` (stable in the model with a large margin);
2. step test (`seq`), log, compare with the notebook;
3. increase $K_d$, then $K_p$, in steps of about 25 %, logging each run;
4. add $K_i$ last, to remove the offset caused by $h$ and motor mismatch;
5. stop at the gains that meet the spec or when the response gets noisy.

## 6. Safety as a control-engineering problem

Every layer in [hardware/rig/safety.md](../../hardware/rig/safety.md) answers a specific
failure: a software bug (e-stop in the power path), a loose wire (pot plausibility check),
a controller that diverges (trip at the stops), a power-up with the switch left on (arm
only on an OFF→ON transition), a slow ESC start-up (3 s boot delay), an unexpected
transient (2 s throttle ramp and a hard cap of 1600 µs). Notebook exercise 3.5 implements
the arming logic; the sketch uses the same numbers, and the test-suite checks that both
stay identical.

## Common mistakes

- Props on the wrong way round or a motor spinning backwards: the beam is pushed *down*.
  Check direction props-off, and which way the air goes props-on, at low throttle.
- Forgetting `zero` (pot centre) or the pot sign: the controller pushes the wrong way.
  Tilt the beam by hand and read the `angle` column before arming.
- Typing the simulated "best" gains first.
- Long, unsupported motor wires at the pivot adding friction and torque.
- Logging with the Serial Plotter open (the logger cannot open the port).
